Chapter 01 · The big idea

What is cloud computing?

Cloud computing is renting computing power — servers, storage, databases, networking — over the internet, on demand, instead of buying and running your own hardware. You pay only for what you use, and scale up or down in minutes.

🔑 The mental model

It's the difference between buying a car (you own it, park it, fix it) and calling a ride (it appears when you need it, you pay per trip, someone else maintains it). The cloud is computing-on-tap.

🎬

Real world: Netflix owns almost no servers. Its entire streaming platform — serving 280M+ members — runs on the public cloud (AWS), scaling automatically for the evening rush.

Chapter 02 · Motivation

Why do organisations move to the cloud?

Six forces drive the decision to adopt public cloud — they all trade owning-and-maintaining for renting-and-focusing.

💰
No upfront cost

Swap big hardware purchases (capex) for pay-as-you-go (opex). Trade fixed expense for variable expense.

📈
Elastic scale

Add hundreds of servers for an hour, then give them back. Never guess capacity again.

⚡
Speed & agility

New resources are minutes away, not weeks. Experiment cheaply and ship faster.

🌍
Go global in minutes

Deploy to data centers on multiple continents to put your app close to every user.

🛡️
Reliability built in

Multiple data centers and automatic backups make high availability achievable for everyone.

🎯
Focus on what matters

Stop the “undifferentiated heavy lifting” of racking, patching and cooling. Build product instead.

Chapter 09 · The workhorse

Instances & virtual servers

An instance (AWS calls them EC2) is a virtual server with a defined slice of CPU, memory, storage and bandwidth. Virtualization lets one physical machine host many isolated instances.

🧰 You choose when launching
📍 Region & Availability Zone — where the server physically lives.
📦 Instance type — a size from a menu (more CPU, more memory, GPUs…).
💿 Image — the OS & software the instance boots with (Linux or Windows).
🌐 Every instance gets addresses
🔒 Private IP — for talking to other instances inside the cloud network.
🌍 Public IP — for reaching the instance from the internet (changes if you stop it).
📌 Elastic IP — a static public IP you keep and re-attach, even after a stop/restart.
💡

Cloud hygiene: instances cost money while they run. Terminate what you're not using — and keep your own notes on how you set each service up. That muscle memory is what the assessment rewards.

Chapter 11 · The catch

Cost & vendor lock-in

The cloud is powerful, but leaning entirely on one provider carries real risks. Knowing them is half of designing well.

⚠️ Risks of relying on one provider
🚫Outages — services can be unavailable for minutes or hours.
💥Data loss — a catastrophic failure could lose data permanently.
💸Price hikes — the provider may raise prices over time.
🔒Lock-in — moving your data elsewhere can be slow and expensive.
🛡️ Storage diversity — the fix

Replicate data across multiple providers, much like RAID spreads data across disks. If one provider fails or hikes prices, your data still lives elsewhere.

Chapter 12 · The planet

Energy & the environment

Data centers consume enormous amounts of electricity for computing and cooling. Efficiency isn't just green — it's a core design concern.

~1.5%
of electricity consumption already went to US data centers as far back as 2006
×2
energy use was expected to double in just five years as demand grew
50%
of full-load power can be drawn by a server even when it sits idle
⚖️ Energy-proportional systems

The ideal machine would draw no power when idle and gradually more as load rises — always running at 100% efficiency. Real servers fall short: even idle, they can waste half their full-load power. That mismatch (Google clusters often sit at just 10–50% CPU use) is exactly what efficiency engineering targets.

🧍

A human is a great approximation — about 70 W at rest, ~120 W on average, but able to surge to 1,000–2,000 W during brief, intense effort. Power scales with the work being done.

Chapter 13 · Contracts & security

SLAs & shared responsibility

📜 Service Level Agreement

A negotiated contract between customer and provider. It defines what service you get (not how it's delivered) — resources, security, timing and quality of service.

✔️ Clarifies who's responsible when things fail
✔️ Reduces conflict & unrealistic expectations
